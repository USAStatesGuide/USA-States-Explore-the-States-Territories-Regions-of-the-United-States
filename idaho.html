About Idaho ▼ +

Idaho is a state in the northwestern United States. It is bordered by Washington and Oregon to the west, Nevada and Utah to the south, Wyoming and Montana to the east, and Canada to the north. The capital and largest city of Idaho is Boise.

Idaho became the 43rd state of the United States on July 3, 1890. The state is known for its mountainous landscapes, forests, rivers, lakes, canyons, agricultural regions, and extensive areas of wilderness.

Geography of Idaho ▼ +

Idaho is characterized by rugged mountain ranges, high plateaus, deep river canyons, and the broad Snake River Plain. The central and northern parts of the state contain sections of the Rocky Mountains, including the Sawtooth, Bitterroot, Clearwater, and Salmon River Mountains. The central mountains are especially rugged, with narrow valleys, alpine lakes, and some of the largest wilderness areas in the contiguous United States.

The Snake River Plain forms a distinctive east–west corridor across southern Idaho and is the state's main agricultural and population belt. Much of its landscape was shaped by ancient volcanic activity, leaving lava fields, basalt formations, and broad fertile areas supported by irrigation. The Snake River cuts through this region and forms dramatic canyons, including the famous Snake River Canyon near Twin Falls, while the state's northern landscapes become increasingly mountainous and forested toward the Canadian border.

Idaho Borders ▼ +

Idaho shares borders with six U.S. states: Washington and Oregon to the west, Nevada and Utah to the south, and Wyoming and Montana to the east.

Idaho also has an international border with Canada to the north. The Canadian province of British Columbia lies along Idaho's northern boundary.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in Idaho is Borah Peak, also known as Mount Borah. It reaches approximately 12,668 feet (3,861 meters) above sea level.

Borah Peak is located in the Lost River Range in central Idaho and is the highest mountain in the state.

Rivers and Waterways ▼ +

Idaho has an extensive network of rivers and streams. Major waterways include the Snake River, Salmon River, Clearwater River, Payette River, Boise River, and St. Joe River.

These waterways are important for agriculture, hydroelectric power, fisheries, ecosystems, recreation, water resources, and communities throughout the state.

Economy of Idaho ▼ +

Idaho has a diverse economy in which agriculture remains especially important. The Snake River Plain is a major farming region, supported by irrigation and known for potatoes, sugar beets, wheat, barley, and other crops. The state also has significant cattle and dairy production, while food processing adds value to its agricultural products.

Idaho also has important industries in semiconductor manufacturing and technology, particularly in the Boise area, as well as lumber and wood products supported by the state's extensive forests. Mining has contributed gold, silver, phosphate, and other minerals, while tourism and outdoor recreation benefit from Idaho's ski areas, mountain landscapes, rivers, lakes, and wilderness. Healthcare, education, construction, and other services also play major roles in the state's economy.

Administrative Division ▼ +

Idaho is divided into 44 counties. Counties are an important level of local government within the state and provide various public services and administrative functions.

The state also contains cities, towns, municipalities, unincorporated communities, and other local areas. Boise is the state capital and the largest city by population.

Major Cities ▼ +

Major cities and population centers in Idaho include Boise, Meridian, Nampa, Idaho Falls, Pocatello, and Caldwell.

Boise is the state capital and largest city. Meridian and Nampa are major population centers in the Treasure Valley. Idaho Falls is an important regional center in eastern Idaho, while Pocatello is an important transportation and educational center in southeastern Idaho.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Idaho's natural environment is strongly shaped by the Rocky Mountains, the Snake River Plain, and the state's extensive forests. The Sawtooth and Bitterroot mountain ranges contain rugged peaks, alpine lakes, and high-elevation habitats, while northern Idaho is known for dense coniferous forests. In the south, the Snake River Plain includes broad valleys, sagebrush landscapes, volcanic terrain, and river corridors.

The Snake River and its tributaries provide important habitats for fish and wildlife, while lakes and mountain streams support a variety of aquatic ecosystems. Idaho is home to species such as elk, mule deer, moose, black bears, mountain goats, and bighorn sheep, as well as trout and salmon in its rivers. Protected areas such as Sawtooth National Recreation Area and parts of the Frank Church–River of No Return Wilderness preserve some of the state's most distinctive mountain, forest, and river landscapes.
