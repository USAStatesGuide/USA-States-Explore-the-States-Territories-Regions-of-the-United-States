Indiana, United States

About Indiana ▼ +

Indiana is a state in the Midwestern United States. It is bordered by Michigan and Lake Michigan to the north, Ohio to the east, Kentucky to the south, and Illinois to the west. The capital and largest city of Indiana is Indianapolis.

Indiana became the 19th state of the United States on December 11, 1816. The state is known for its agricultural lands, manufacturing industries, forests, rivers, lakes, and important transportation networks. Indiana is also widely known for the Indianapolis 500 automobile race.

Geography of Indiana ▼ +

Indiana lies at the meeting point of the Great Lakes and the interior Midwest. Northern Indiana reaches Lake Michigan and includes the distinctive Indiana Dunes landscape of sandy beaches, dunes, wetlands, and low wooded areas. Much of central and northern Indiana consists of broad, fertile plains shaped by glaciers, with gently rolling farmland extending across the Corn Belt.

The Wabash River is the state's principal river and flows across much of western and central Indiana before joining the Ohio River. Southern Indiana has a noticeably different landscape, with wooded hills, narrow valleys, limestone formations, and caves, particularly near the Ohio River. Glacial deposits dominate much of the north, while the unglaciated southern hills give the state a clear geographic transition from flat plains to more rugged terrain.

Indiana Borders ▼ +

Indiana shares land borders with Michigan to the north, Ohio to the east, and Illinois to the west. Its southern boundary is shared with Kentucky, much of which follows the Ohio River.

Indiana also borders Lake Michigan to the northwest. The lake provides Indiana with access to the Great Lakes and forms part of the state's northern geographic boundary.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest natural point in Indiana is Hoosier Hill, which reaches approximately 1,257 feet (383 meters) above sea level.

Hoosier Hill is located in Wayne County in eastern Indiana, near the Ohio border. Although it is the state's highest point, the surrounding terrain is relatively low compared with mountainous regions of the United States.

Rivers and Waterways ▼ +

Indiana has an extensive network of rivers and streams. Major waterways include the Wabash River, White River, Ohio River, Tippecanoe River, St. Joseph River, and Maumee River.

These waterways are important for ecosystems, agriculture, recreation, water resources, wildlife habitats, and transportation. The Ohio River forms much of Indiana's southern boundary and connects the state with the larger Mississippi River watershed.

Economy of Indiana ▼ +

Indiana has a strong manufacturing economy, particularly in automobile production, steel, machinery, pharmaceuticals, and advanced industrial equipment. The northern part of the state has long been associated with steelmaking around the Lake Michigan shoreline, while automobile and parts manufacturing is concentrated in several communities across the state. Indiana is also an important center for pharmaceutical research and production, with major activity around Indianapolis.

Agriculture remains important across Indiana's fertile central and northern plains, where corn and soybeans are the leading crops, alongside hogs, cattle, poultry, and dairy production. The state's location between the Great Lakes and major Midwestern markets gives it a major advantage in transportation and logistics. Extensive interstate highways, railways, and access to Lake Michigan and the Ohio River support the movement of manufactured goods, agricultural products, and other freight.

Administrative Division ▼ +

Indiana is divided into 92 counties. Counties are an important level of local government within the state and perform various administrative and public service functions.

The state also contains cities, towns, townships, unincorporated communities, and other local areas. Indianapolis is the state capital and the largest city by population.

Major Cities ▼ +

Major cities and population centers in Indiana include Indianapolis, Fort Wayne, Evansville, South Bend, Carmel, and Fishers.

Indianapolis is the state capital and largest city and is a major center for government, business, healthcare, education, sports, and transportation. Fort Wayne is a major regional center in northeastern Indiana, while Evansville serves as an important center in the state's southwestern region.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Indiana's natural environment reflects its position between the Great Lakes, the central Midwest, and the wooded hills of the south. Along Lake Michigan, the Indiana Dunes contain a distinctive mix of shifting sand dunes, beaches, wetlands, oak savannas, forests, and grasslands. Central Indiana is dominated by fertile plains that were once covered extensively by tallgrass prairie, while many remaining natural areas now survive in parks, preserves, and along river corridors.

Southern Indiana has a more rugged landscape, especially around the Knobstone Escarpment and the limestone country near the Ohio River. Caves, springs, wooded valleys, and exposed limestone formations create habitats different from those of the northern plains. Rivers such as the Wabash, White, and Ohio, together with wetlands and forests, support white-tailed deer, wild turkeys, waterfowl, fish, amphibians, and many other species.
