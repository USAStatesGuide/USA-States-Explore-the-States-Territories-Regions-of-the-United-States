About U.S. Virgin Islands ▼ +

The U.S. Virgin Islands are an unincorporated organized territory of the United States in the Caribbean Sea. The territory is located east of Puerto Rico and forms part of the Virgin Islands archipelago. The capital and largest city is Charlotte Amalie on the island of St. Thomas.

The three principal inhabited islands are St. Thomas, St. John, and St. Croix. The territory also includes numerous smaller islands and cays. The U.S. Virgin Islands are known for their tropical climate, mountainous terrain, beaches, coral reefs, bays, and important marine ecosystems.

Geography of U.S. Virgin Islands ▼ +

The U.S. Virgin Islands are located in the northeastern Caribbean Sea, east of Puerto Rico and west of the British Virgin Islands. The territory consists of mountainous islands surrounded by numerous smaller islands, cays, bays, and coastal waters.

St. Thomas and St. John are part of the northern island group and have rugged, hilly landscapes. St. Croix lies farther south and is the largest of the three main islands. The islands contain steep hills, valleys, coastal plains, limestone formations, beaches, coral reefs, and tropical vegetation.

Borders and Location ▼ +

The U.S. Virgin Islands are surrounded by the Caribbean Sea and the Atlantic Ocean region. They lie east of Puerto Rico and immediately west of the British Virgin Islands.

Because the U.S. Virgin Islands are an island territory, they do not share land borders with U.S. states or other U.S. territories. Their geographic position gives them close maritime and cultural connections with Puerto Rico, the British Virgin Islands, and the wider Caribbean region.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in the U.S. Virgin Islands is Crown Mountain on St. Thomas, which reaches approximately 1,555 feet (474 meters) above sea level.

Crown Mountain is located on the mountainous island of St. Thomas and is the highest natural elevation in the territory. The island's steep terrain contributes to its distinctive landscape of ridges, valleys, and coastal slopes.

Waterways and Coastal Areas ▼ +

The U.S. Virgin Islands do not have large permanent rivers because of their small size and tropical island geography. Instead, the islands contain short seasonal streams, drainage channels, springs, bays, coves, wetlands, and groundwater systems.

Coastal waters are especially important to the territory. Coral reefs, seagrass beds, mangroves, bays, and surrounding marine habitats support biodiversity and contribute to fishing, tourism, recreation, transportation, and coastal protection.

Economy of U.S. Virgin Islands ▼ +

The economy of the U.S. Virgin Islands is strongly based on tourism, hospitality, retail, transportation, services, and government. Tourism is particularly important because of the territory's beaches, tropical climate, historical sites, cruise ports, and marine environments.

St. Thomas is an important center for tourism, commerce, transportation, and government. St. Croix has a more diversified economic base that includes tourism, services, agriculture, and industrial activities. St. John is especially known for tourism and protected natural areas, including much of the island's land within Virgin Islands National Park.

Administrative Division ▼ +

The U.S. Virgin Islands are divided into two main administrative districts: St. Croix and St. Thomas-St. John.

The territory does not use counties in the same way as U.S. states. St. Thomas and St. John are administered together as one district, while St. Croix forms a separate district. Local communities and towns are distributed across the three principal inhabited islands.

Major Cities and Population Centers ▼ +

Major population centers in the U.S. Virgin Islands include Charlotte Amalie on St. Thomas, Christiansted on St. Croix, Frederiksted on St. Croix, and communities on St. John such as Cruz Bay.

Charlotte Amalie is the capital and an important center for government, commerce, tourism, transportation, and cruise activity. Christiansted and Frederiksted are important historic and commercial communities on St. Croix, while Cruz Bay serves as the principal population and transportation center of St. John.

Natural Environment ▼ +

The natural environment of the U.S. Virgin Islands includes tropical forests, hills, valleys, beaches, coral reefs, mangroves, seagrass beds, wetlands, and coastal ecosystems. The islands have warm tropical conditions and receive seasonal rainfall influenced by their Caribbean location.

Marine ecosystems are particularly important and support coral communities, fish, sea turtles, seabirds, and many other species. St. John contains extensive protected natural areas, while the surrounding waters provide important habitats for marine life. The territory's beaches, forests, reefs, and coastal environments also play a major role in tourism and recreation.
