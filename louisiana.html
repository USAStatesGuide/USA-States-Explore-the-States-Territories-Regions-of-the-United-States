Louisiana, United States

About Louisiana ▼ +

Louisiana is a state in the southern United States, located along the northern coast of the Gulf of Mexico. It borders Texas to the west, Arkansas to the north, Mississippi to the east, and the Gulf of Mexico to the south. The capital of Louisiana is Baton Rouge.

Louisiana became the 18th state of the United States on April 30, 1812. The state is known for its low-lying coastal plains, wetlands, bayous, rivers, forests, and the Mississippi River. Its cultural heritage reflects French, Spanish, African, Caribbean, and other influences.

Geography of Louisiana ▼ +

Louisiana is one of the lowest-lying states in the United States, with a landscape strongly shaped by the Mississippi River and its delta. Southern Louisiana contains vast coastal marshes, swamps, bayous, mudflats, and shallow lakes, while the Mississippi River and its distributaries have built layers of sediment across the lower part of the state. The Atchafalaya Basin forms one of the largest river swamps in the country and is a major geographic feature of south-central Louisiana.

The Louisiana coast extends along the Gulf of Mexico and includes barrier islands, coastal bays, estuaries, and rapidly changing wetlands. Farther north, the terrain gradually becomes higher and less dominated by coastal water, with forests, agricultural land, and gently rolling uplands. The Red River and Ouachita River shape important valleys in northern Louisiana, while the Mississippi River forms much of the state's eastern boundary before reaching the Gulf through its extensive delta system.

Louisiana Borders ▼ +

Louisiana shares land borders with Texas to the west, Arkansas to the north, and Mississippi to the east.

To the south, Louisiana borders the Gulf of Mexico. Its extensive coastline includes bays, marshes, barrier islands, and coastal wetlands.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in Louisiana is Driskill Mountain, which reaches approximately 535 feet (163 meters) above sea level.

Driskill Mountain is located in Bienville Parish in the northern part of the state. It is the highest natural point in Louisiana.

Rivers and Waterways ▼ +

Louisiana has an extensive network of rivers, bayous, lakes, and wetlands. Major waterways include the Mississippi River, Red River, Atchafalaya River, Ouachita River, Sabine River, and Calcasieu River.

These waterways are important for transportation, fisheries, agriculture, wildlife habitats, flood management, industry, recreation, and the state's coastal ecosystems.

Economy of Louisiana ▼ +

Louisiana's economy is closely tied to its location along the lower Mississippi River and the Gulf of Mexico. Oil and natural gas remain important, supporting offshore production, petroleum refining, petrochemical manufacturing, and related industries concentrated along the Mississippi River between Baton Rouge and New Orleans. The state's major ports also handle large volumes of international cargo, making Louisiana an important gateway for energy, agricultural products, and industrial goods.

Agriculture and seafood production are particularly suited to Louisiana's warm climate, fertile river sediments, and coastal waters. The state is a major producer of rice, sugarcane, soybeans, and crawfish, while shrimp, oysters, and other seafood support coastal communities. Louisiana also has important chemical, aerospace, manufacturing, and transportation industries, while tourism and hospitality benefit from New Orleans, the state's distinctive culture, cuisine, music, and historic communities.

Administrative Division ▼ +

Louisiana is divided into 64 parishes, which serve a role similar to counties in most other U.S. states.

The state also contains municipalities, cities, towns, villages, and other local governmental units. New Orleans has a consolidated city-parish government known as the City of New Orleans and Orleans Parish.

Major Cities ▼ +

Major cities and population centers in Louisiana include New Orleans, Baton Rouge, Shreveport, Lafayette, Lake Charles, and Kenner.

Baton Rouge is the state capital. New Orleans is the largest city by population and is a major cultural, tourism, port, and economic center. Shreveport is an important population and economic center in northern Louisiana.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Louisiana's natural environment is dominated by water, with extensive coastal marshes, cypress swamps, bayous, floodplain forests, and shallow lakes. The Atchafalaya Basin is one of the state's most distinctive ecosystems, containing vast bottomland forests, swamps, and winding waterways. Farther south, the Mississippi River Delta creates a complex network of freshwater and salt marshes, estuaries, barrier islands, and coastal habitats along the Gulf of Mexico.

These wetlands provide breeding and feeding grounds for fish, shrimp, oysters, waterfowl, and many other species, while coastal marshes help reduce the effects of waves and storm surges. Louisiana is also home to American alligators, brown pelicans, herons, egrets, turtles, and numerous migratory birds. The Mississippi and Atchafalaya river systems continually transport sediment and water through the state's wetlands, although coastal erosion, subsidence, and rising sea levels are major challenges for these ecosystems.
