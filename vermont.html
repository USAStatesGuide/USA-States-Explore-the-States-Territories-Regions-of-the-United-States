Vermont, United States

About Vermont ▼ +

Vermont is a state in the northeastern region of the United States. It is bordered by Canada to the north, New Hampshire to the east, Massachusetts to the south, and New York to the west. The capital of Vermont is Montpelier.

Vermont became the 14th state of the United States on March 4, 1791. The state is known for its Green Mountains, forests, lakes, rivers, rural landscapes, agricultural lands, and distinctive New England communities.

Geography of Vermont ▼ +

Vermont is dominated by the Green Mountains, which run roughly north to south through the center of the state and divide its major geographic regions. The mountains contain forested ridges, narrow valleys, high elevations, and numerous streams, while the western side descends toward the broad Champlain Valley. Eastern Vermont is more rugged and hilly, with the Connecticut River forming an important geographic boundary and transportation corridor.

Lake Champlain is one of Vermont's defining geographic features and forms much of the state's western boundary with New York. Its shoreline includes bays, wetlands, islands, and low agricultural plains, creating a marked contrast with the nearby Green Mountains. The Connecticut River forms much of the eastern boundary with New Hampshire, while rivers such as the Winooski, Otter Creek, and Lamoille drain the state's valleys toward Lake Champlain or the Connecticut River. This combination of mountains, lake lowlands, river valleys, and forests gives Vermont its distinctive landscape.

Vermont Borders ▼ +

Vermont shares land borders with Canada to the north, New Hampshire to the east, Massachusetts to the south, and New York to the west.

Vermont also has a water boundary with New York across portions of Lake Champlain. The state has no ocean coastline.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in Vermont is Mount Mansfield, which reaches approximately 4,393 feet (1,339 meters) above sea level.

Mount Mansfield is located in the northern Green Mountains. It is the highest natural point in Vermont and is part of a prominent mountain area that includes several distinct peaks and ridgelines.

Rivers and Waterways ▼ +

Vermont has an extensive network of rivers, streams, lakes, and wetlands. Major waterways include the Connecticut River, Winooski River, Otter Creek, Missisquoi River, Lamoille River, and White River.

These waterways are important for ecosystems, agriculture, water resources, recreation, transportation, wildlife, and communities throughout the state. Lake Champlain is the largest lake associated with Vermont and is an important freshwater ecosystem.

Economy of Vermont ▼ +

Vermont's economy is closely connected to agriculture, tourism, manufacturing, forestry, healthcare, education, and outdoor recreation. Dairy farming is an important part of the agricultural sector, with milk and dairy products forming a major share of farm production. Vermont is also nationally known for maple syrup, with sugar maple forests supporting a large seasonal maple industry. Food and beverage production, including specialty cheeses and other locally produced foods, adds value to the state's agricultural economy.

Tourism is especially important in Vermont's mountain communities, with ski resorts, hiking trails, lakes, covered bridges, and scenic rural landscapes attracting visitors throughout the year. The Green Mountains support a significant winter recreation industry, while Lake Champlain and the state's forests contribute to summer tourism. Manufacturing includes precision equipment, electronics, food products, and other specialized goods, while forestry and wood products remain important in rural areas. Healthcare, education, and professional services provide major sources of employment, particularly in the state's larger towns and cities.

Administrative Division ▼ +

Vermont is divided into 14 counties. Counties are an important level of local government within the state.

The state also contains cities, towns, villages, unincorporated communities, and other local areas. Vermont is particularly known for its town-based local government structure.

Major Cities ▼ +

Major cities and population centers in Vermont include Burlington, South Burlington, Rutland, Essex, Colchester, and Bennington.

Montpelier is the state capital, while Burlington is the largest city by population and an important center for education, healthcare, business, culture, and commerce. South Burlington and surrounding communities form an important population and economic center in the Champlain Valley.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Vermont's natural environment is dominated by the forested Green Mountains and the surrounding valleys and hills. Northern hardwoods such as sugar maple, yellow birch, and beech cover much of the mountain landscape, while higher elevations contain spruce and fir forests. These forests are especially important for biodiversity, recreation, and maple syrup production, with large areas of woodland managed for both conservation and sustainable use.

Lake Champlain and its surrounding wetlands form an important freshwater ecosystem, supporting fish, waterfowl, shorebirds, and other aquatic wildlife. Vermont's mountain streams, ponds, and wetlands provide additional habitats for beavers, river otters, white-tailed deer, black bears, moose, and numerous bird species. The state's forests and waterways also create important ecological corridors between the Green Mountains, Champlain Valley, and the Connecticut River watershed.
