About Wake Island ▼ +

Wake Island is a remote coral atoll in the central Pacific Ocean and an unincorporated territory of the United States. It is located approximately 2,300 miles west of Honolulu, Hawaii, and about 1,500 miles east of Guam.

The atoll consists of three main islands: Wake Island, Wilkes Island, and Peale Island. They surround a shallow central lagoon and are connected by narrow land areas and coral reef formations. Wake Island is internationally known for its strategic location, aviation history, military history, and surrounding marine environment.

Geography of Wake Island ▼ +

Wake Island is a low-lying coral atoll formed around a submerged volcanic structure. It consists of coral reef, sandy coastal areas, three main islands, and a large shallow lagoon in the center of the atoll.

Wake Island is the largest of the three main islands and contains most of the atoll's infrastructure, including the airfield. Wilkes Island and Peale Island are smaller land areas located around the lagoon and are characterized by coastal vegetation, sandy areas, and coral formations.

Location and Surroundings ▼ +

Wake Island is located in the central Pacific Ocean, approximately 2,300 miles west of Honolulu and about 1,500 miles east of Guam. It lies far from the main Hawaiian Islands and other large land areas.

The atoll is surrounded by open ocean and extensive coral reef habitats. Its isolated position has given it strategic importance for aviation, communications, transportation, and military activities in the Pacific.

Highest Point ▼ +

Wake Island is extremely low-lying and has no mountains or significant natural high points. The highest natural areas of the atoll rise only a few meters above sea level.

The low elevation is characteristic of coral atolls and makes Wake Island particularly sensitive to coastal erosion, severe storms, flooding, and long-term changes in sea level.

Lagoon and Waterways ▼ +

The central lagoon is the dominant water feature of Wake Island. It is enclosed by the three main islands and surrounding coral reef structures and provides sheltered marine habitat for fish, seabirds, corals, and other aquatic organisms.

The surrounding coral reef and ocean waters form part of the larger marine ecosystem of the central Pacific. Unlike continental regions, Wake Island has no natural rivers or freshwater river systems.

History of Wake Island ▼ +

Wake Island was claimed by the United States in the nineteenth century and was formally annexed by the United States in 1899. Its location in the central Pacific later made it important for communications, aviation, and transportation.

Wake Island became especially important during World War II. In December 1941, Japanese forces attacked and captured the island after a period of fighting between United States and Japanese forces. The Battle of Wake Island became an important episode in the early Pacific War.

After World War II, Wake Island continued to be used for aviation, military, and logistical purposes. Its airfield remained strategically important because of the island's isolated location between Hawaii and Asia.

Administrative Status ▼ +

Wake Island is an unincorporated territory of the United States. It is not a U.S. state and does not have counties, cities, or a permanent civilian government structure like the states of the mainland.

The island is administered by the United States government and is primarily associated with military, aviation, logistical, and other authorized activities. Access to the island is restricted.

Major Settlements ▼ +

Wake Island has no permanent civilian cities or towns. Wake Island itself contains the principal infrastructure and facilities of the atoll, including the airfield and areas used for authorized operations.

The island may have a small temporary population consisting of military personnel, contractors, maintenance workers, and other authorized personnel. The population can change depending on operational requirements.

Marine Environment ▼ +

The waters surrounding Wake Island contain extensive coral reef habitats and support a diverse marine ecosystem. The lagoon, reef flats, reef slopes, and surrounding ocean provide habitat for numerous marine species.

Marine life around Wake Island includes coral communities, reef fish, seabirds, and other marine organisms. The relatively isolated location of the atoll contributes to the importance of its surrounding marine environment.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Wake Island has a tropical coastal environment consisting of sandy beaches, coral reefs, shallow lagoon waters, coastal vegetation, and surrounding ocean habitats. The isolated atoll provides habitat for a variety of seabirds and marine wildlife.

The islands support nesting and resting areas for seabirds, while the lagoon and surrounding reefs provide habitat for fish, corals, and other marine organisms. The small size and low elevation of the atoll make its natural environment particularly sensitive to storms, erosion, and environmental changes.

The combination of coral reefs, lagoon waters, sandy beaches, coastal vegetation, and marine habitats makes Wake Island an important ecological area in the central Pacific.

Conservation ▼ +

Wake Island and its surrounding waters contain important terrestrial and marine habitats. Environmental management activities focus on protecting wildlife, maintaining native habitats, controlling invasive species, and reducing the environmental impacts of human activities.

Conservation efforts also help protect coral reef ecosystems, seabird habitats, coastal vegetation, and other natural resources of the atoll. Because Wake Island is small, isolated, and low-lying, environmental protection is an important part of its management.
