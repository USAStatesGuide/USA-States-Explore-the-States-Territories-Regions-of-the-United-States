Mississippi, United States

About Mississippi ▼ +

Mississippi is a state in the southeastern region of the United States. It is bordered by Tennessee to the north, Alabama to the east, the Gulf of Mexico and Louisiana to the south, and Louisiana and Arkansas to the west across the Mississippi River. The capital of Mississippi is Jackson.

Mississippi became the 20th state of the United States on December 10, 1817. The state is known for its forests, rivers, agricultural lands, wetlands, coastal areas, and diverse landscapes extending from the Mississippi River Delta to the Gulf Coast.

Geography of Mississippi ▼ +

Mississippi is strongly shaped by the Mississippi River and its broad floodplain along the western side of the state. The Mississippi Delta is a remarkably flat and fertile region formed from river sediments and is one of the state's most distinctive landscapes. Farther east, the terrain rises into the loess-covered hills and rolling uplands of central and northern Mississippi, while the Piney Woods region in the south is characterized by sandy soils and extensive pine forests.

Southern Mississippi reaches the Gulf of Mexico and has a low, gently sloping coastal landscape with bays, tidal marshes, estuaries, and barrier islands. The Pascagoula River system is a major feature of the southeastern coast, while the Mississippi Sound separates the mainland from several barrier islands. The state's geography therefore changes from the vast Mississippi River floodplain in the west to wooded uplands and Gulf Coast wetlands in the south.

Mississippi Borders ▼ +

Mississippi shares land borders with Tennessee to the north, Alabama to the east, and Louisiana to the south and southwest. It also borders Arkansas to the northwest across the Mississippi River.

Mississippi also has a coastline along the Gulf of Mexico in the southern part of the state.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in Mississippi is Woodall Mountain, which reaches approximately 807 feet (246 meters) above sea level.

Woodall Mountain is located in Tishomingo County in the northeastern part of Mississippi. It is the highest natural point in the state.

Rivers and Waterways ▼ +

Mississippi has an extensive network of rivers, streams, lakes, wetlands, and coastal waterways. Major rivers include the Mississippi River, Yazoo River, Pearl River, Pascagoula River, Big Black River, and Tombigbee River.

These waterways are important for ecosystems, transportation, agriculture, water resources, fisheries, recreation, flood management, and communities throughout the state.

Economy of Mississippi ▼ +

Mississippi's economy has strong connections to agriculture, manufacturing, forestry, energy, and the Gulf Coast. The fertile Mississippi Delta supports large-scale production of cotton, soybeans, corn, rice, and other crops, while the state's poultry industry is one of its major agricultural activities. Mississippi is also well known for farm-raised catfish, particularly in the Delta region. Forestry and wood products are especially important in the southern Piney Woods.

Manufacturing has expanded through automobile production, aerospace, electronics, chemicals, and other advanced industries. The Gulf Coast is home to important shipbuilding and maritime activities, including major facilities around Pascagoula, while ports support movement of industrial and agricultural goods. Energy production, healthcare, education, transportation, and tourism also contribute to the economy, with the state's Gulf beaches, river landscapes, historic sites, and cultural heritage attracting visitors.

Administrative Division ▼ +

Mississippi is divided into 82 counties. Counties are an important level of local government within the state.

The state also contains cities, towns, villages, municipalities, and other local communities. Counties and municipalities provide various local governmental services to residents.

Major Cities ▼ +

Major cities and population centers in Mississippi include Jackson, Gulfport, Southaven, Hattiesburg, Biloxi, and Meridian.

Jackson is the state capital and largest city by population. It is an important center for government, healthcare, education, business, and culture. Gulfport and Biloxi are important Gulf Coast cities, while Hattiesburg and Meridian serve as major regional centers for education, healthcare, commerce, and transportation.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Mississippi's natural environment changes from the river floodplains of the west to the pine forests of the south and the Gulf Coast. The Mississippi Delta contains wetlands, backwater areas, and floodplain habitats shaped by the river, while the southern Piney Woods are dominated by longleaf and other pine forests. Rivers such as the Pascagoula and Pearl create important freshwater habitats, wetlands, and forested corridors across the state.

Along the Gulf of Mexico, Mississippi has salt marshes, tidal estuaries, shallow coastal waters, and barrier islands that provide important habitat for fish, shrimp, oysters, shorebirds, and other wildlife. The Mississippi Sound and coastal wetlands also serve as nursery areas for marine species. Inland forests support white-tailed deer, wild turkeys, black bears, river otters, and numerous bird species, while the state's rivers and wetlands provide important habitats for turtles, frogs, fish, and migratory waterfowl.
