About Washington, D.C. ▼ +

Washington, D.C., formally the District of Columbia, is the capital of the United States. It is a federal district located on the Potomac River between Maryland and Virginia. Washington, D.C. is not a state and serves as the seat of the federal government.

The federal district was established to serve as the national capital, and the District of Columbia was organized under federal authority in 1801. Washington, D.C. is known for its monuments, museums, government institutions, historic neighborhoods, parks, and distinctive urban landscape along the Potomac and Anacostia Rivers.

Washington, D.C. vs. Washington State ▼ +

Washington, D.C. and Washington State are two different places in the United States. Washington, D.C. is the federal capital and is a federal district located on the East Coast, between Maryland and Virginia. Washington State is a U.S. state located in the Pacific Northwest on the West Coast.

Washington, D.C. is not a state and does not belong to any state. It is the seat of the federal government and contains the White House, the U.S. Capitol, and many other federal institutions. Washington State, on the other hand, is one of the 50 states and has Olympia as its capital, while Seattle is its largest city.

The two places are also very far apart geographically. Washington, D.C. is in the eastern United States near the Atlantic region, whereas Washington State is in the far northwest, bordering Oregon, Idaho, and the Pacific Ocean, as well as the Canadian province of British Columbia.

Geography of Washington, D.C. ▼ +

Washington, D.C. occupies a compact area in the Mid-Atlantic region along the Potomac River. Its terrain includes low coastal plain areas, rolling uplands, river terraces, wooded slopes, and urbanized hills. The northwest part of the District is generally higher and more elevated, while lower elevations occur toward the Potomac and Anacostia Rivers.

The District is organized around the U.S. Capitol and is divided into four quadrants: Northwest, Northeast, Southwest, and Southeast. The Potomac River forms much of the boundary with Virginia, while Maryland surrounds the District on its northern and eastern sides. Despite its small size, Washington, D.C. contains a varied mixture of dense urban areas, parks, waterways, and wooded landscapes.

Washington, D.C. Borders ▼ +

Washington, D.C. borders the state of Maryland on its north and east sides. The Potomac River forms much of its western and southwestern boundary with Virginia.

The District has extensive waterfront along the Potomac River and the Anacostia River.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest elevations in Washington, D.C. are found in the northwest part of the District. The high point is in the Tenleytown area, with an elevation of roughly 400–414 feet (122–126 meters) above sea level.

The northwest uplands are part of the Piedmont physiographic region and are noticeably higher than the low-lying river and coastal areas of the District.

Rivers and Waterways ▼ +

Washington, D.C. is centered on two major rivers: the Potomac River and the Anacostia River. Other important waterways and streams include Rock Creek and the remnants of historic Tiber Creek.

These waterways shape the District's landscape and support wetlands, aquatic habitats, recreation, waterfront parks, transportation, and ecological functions. The Potomac also connects Washington, D.C. to the wider Chesapeake Bay watershed.

Economy of Washington, D.C. ▼ +

Washington, D.C. has an economy strongly connected to the federal government, professional and business services, tourism, education, healthcare, hospitality, technology, and international organizations. The District's role as the national capital makes government and public-sector activity especially important to its economic structure.

Washington, D.C. is also a major center for law, consulting, communications, research, higher education, museums, cultural institutions, and international affairs. Tourism is supported by national monuments, museums, historic sites, and government landmarks visited by millions of people.

Administrative Division ▼ +

Washington, D.C. does not have county governments. For statistical purposes, the Census Bureau treats the District as both a state-equivalent area and a county-equivalent area, while the District government performs municipal and county-type functions.

The District is divided into 8 wards for local representation and governance. It also contains neighborhoods, Advisory Neighborhood Commissions, and other local areas that help organize civic and community life.

Major Areas and Neighborhoods ▼ +

Because Washington, D.C. is a single consolidated city-district, it does not contain separate incorporated cities. Major neighborhoods and areas include Downtown, Capitol Hill, Georgetown, Dupont Circle, Adams Morgan, Columbia Heights, and Anacostia.

Washington, D.C. is the national capital and the center of the federal government. Its neighborhoods vary considerably, ranging from historic residential districts and commercial centers to waterfront communities and densely developed downtown areas.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Washington, D.C.'s natural environment includes river corridors, wetlands, wooded parks, streams, urban forests, and areas of Piedmont terrain. Important natural areas include Rock Creek Park, the Potomac waterfront, the Anacostia River corridor, and numerous smaller parks and green spaces throughout the District.

The District's parks, wetlands, rivers, and urban green spaces provide habitat for birds, mammals, reptiles, amphibians, fish, insects, and native plants. These environments also help manage stormwater, reduce urban heat, protect waterways, and provide recreation and ecological benefits within the nation's capital.
