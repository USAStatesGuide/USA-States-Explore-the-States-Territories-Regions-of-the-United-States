About Northern Mariana Islands ▼ +

The Commonwealth of the Northern Mariana Islands (CNMI) is a self-governing U.S. territory in the western Pacific Ocean. It is part of the Mariana Islands archipelago and lies north of Guam. The capital and principal population center is Saipan.

The Northern Mariana Islands consist of 14 islands extending northward from Guam. The three main permanently inhabited islands are Saipan, Tinian, and Rota. The islands are known for their volcanic origins, limestone formations, tropical climate, coral reefs, beaches, and marine environments.

Geography of Northern Mariana Islands ▼ +

The Northern Mariana Islands form a chain of 14 islands in the western Pacific Ocean, extending northward from Guam. The islands are part of the Mariana Archipelago, which lies along the western edge of the Pacific Ocean near the Mariana Trench.

The islands have volcanic origins and include rugged uplands, limestone terraces, coastal plains, cliffs, beaches, coral reefs, lagoons, and tropical forests. Saipan is the largest and most populated island, while Tinian and Rota are the other major permanently inhabited islands.

Borders and Location ▼ +

The Northern Mariana Islands are located in the western Pacific Ocean. They are situated north of Guam and form the northern part of the Mariana Islands archipelago.

Because the Northern Mariana Islands are an island territory, they do not share land borders with other U.S. states or territories. The islands are surrounded by the Pacific Ocean and are closely associated geographically with Guam and the wider Micronesian region.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in the Northern Mariana Islands is Mount Tapochau on Saipan, which rises to approximately 1,545 feet (471 meters) above sea level.

Mount Tapochau is located near the center of Saipan and provides elevated views across much of the island and its surrounding Pacific waters.

Rivers and Waterways ▼ +

The Northern Mariana Islands do not have large river systems comparable to those found in mainland states. Their small island landscapes are characterized mainly by short streams, seasonal drainage channels, springs, groundwater systems, coastal wetlands, and lagoons.

Freshwater resources are especially important because the islands have limited surface water. Rainfall, groundwater, and aquifers provide important water resources for communities, agriculture, ecosystems, and other activities throughout the islands.

Economy of Northern Mariana Islands ▼ +

The economy of the Northern Mariana Islands is strongly connected to tourism, hospitality, retail, transportation, construction, and agriculture. Tourism has traditionally been one of the most important economic activities, particularly on Saipan.

Agriculture is also important on Tinian and Rota, while fishing and marine activities contribute to local livelihoods. Saipan serves as the principal commercial and governmental center of the commonwealth and contains many of its major businesses, services, and transportation facilities.

Administrative Division ▼ +

The Northern Mariana Islands are divided into four municipalities: Saipan, Tinian, Rota, and the Northern Islands.

Saipan is the main administrative and population center. Tinian and Rota are the other two municipalities with permanent populations, while the Northern Islands municipality includes the more sparsely populated islands farther north in the archipelago.

Major Cities and Population Centers ▼ +

The principal population centers in the Northern Mariana Islands include Saipan, San Jose on Tinian, and settlements on Rota. Saipan is by far the largest population center in the commonwealth.

Saipan is the capital and the main center for government, commerce, tourism, transportation, education, and services. Tinian and Rota have smaller populations and economies that include tourism, agriculture, and local services.

Natural Environment ▼ +

The natural environment of the Northern Mariana Islands includes tropical forests, limestone landscapes, volcanic formations, coral reefs, beaches, coastal wetlands, lagoons, and surrounding marine ecosystems. The islands experience a tropical climate with warm temperatures throughout much of the year.

Coral reefs and coastal waters provide habitats for numerous marine organisms, while forests and other terrestrial habitats support birds, reptiles, insects, and other wildlife. The islands' geographic isolation also contributes to distinctive ecosystems and species. Their coastal and marine environments are important for biodiversity, tourism, fishing, and recreation.
