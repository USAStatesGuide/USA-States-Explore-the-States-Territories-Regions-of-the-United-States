About Howland Island ▼ +

Howland Island is an uninhabited coral island in the central Pacific Ocean. It is an unincorporated, unorganized territory of the United States and forms part of the United States Minor Outlying Islands.

The island has no permanent human population and has no towns, cities, or local government. Howland Island is protected as part of the Howland Island National Wildlife Refuge and is surrounded by important marine habitats. It is one of the remote U.S. islands in the Pacific Ocean.

Geography of Howland Island ▼ +

Howland Island is a small, low-lying coral island located in the central Pacific Ocean near the equator. It lies approximately halfway between Hawaii and Australia and is surrounded by a coral reef and shallow coastal waters.

The island is mostly flat and consists primarily of coral sand, reef limestone, and other materials associated with its tropical marine environment. Howland Island has no mountains, permanent rivers, or large freshwater lakes. Its very low elevation makes the island particularly vulnerable to coastal flooding and changes in sea level.

Location and Surrounding Area ▼ +

Howland Island is located in the central Pacific Ocean, just north of the equator. It is situated approximately 3,100 kilometers (1,930 miles) southwest of Honolulu, Hawaii.

The island has no land borders because it is completely surrounded by the Pacific Ocean. Its nearest neighboring U.S. territory is Baker Island, located to the southwest. The surrounding ocean forms an important part of Howland Island's geographic and ecological environment.

Highest Point ▼ +

Howland Island is extremely low-lying. Its highest natural elevation is approximately 10 feet (3 meters) above sea level.

The island has no significant hills or mountains. Its flat coral landscape is characteristic of low-lying atoll islands in the central Pacific Ocean.

Waterways and Coastal Areas ▼ +

Howland Island has no permanent rivers, streams, or freshwater lakes. Because of its small size and porous coral geology, rainfall quickly infiltrates the ground, leaving no major permanent surface-water systems.

The island is surrounded by coral reefs, shallow coastal waters, and deeper Pacific Ocean waters. These marine environments provide important habitat for fish, corals, seabirds, sea turtles, and other wildlife.

Human Activity and Economy ▼ +

Howland Island has no permanent residents and does not have a conventional local economy. There are no towns, commercial centers, hotels, or permanent businesses on the island.

Human activity is limited and is primarily associated with wildlife management, scientific research, conservation, and occasional authorized visits. The island's protected status limits development and helps preserve its natural environment.

Administrative Status ▼ +

Howland Island is an unincorporated, unorganized territory of the United States. It is administered as part of the United States Minor Outlying Islands rather than as a state, commonwealth, or organized U.S. territory.

The island is federally managed and is protected as the Howland Island National Wildlife Refuge. Because there is no permanent population, Howland Island does not have municipalities, counties, cities, or local elected government.

Settlements and Population ▼ +

Howland Island has no permanent population and has no officially established cities or towns. There are no permanent residential communities on the island.

The island was temporarily inhabited during historical settlement efforts in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries. It was also associated with plans for an aviation facility before World War II. Today, access is restricted and human presence is generally limited to authorized scientific, conservation, and management activities.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Howland Island supports a distinctive tropical coral-island ecosystem. Its vegetation consists mainly of plants adapted to dry, exposed island conditions, while the island provides important nesting and resting habitat for numerous seabirds.

The surrounding marine environment contains coral reefs, fish, sea turtles, and other ocean wildlife. Howland Island is especially important for seabirds and other marine- dependent species. Its protection as a national wildlife refuge helps conserve its terrestrial habitats and surrounding marine ecosystem.

Conservation ▼ +

Howland Island is protected as part of the Howland Island National Wildlife Refuge. The island provides important nesting and resting habitat for numerous seabirds and shorebirds.

Conservation efforts focus on protecting native vegetation, seabird colonies, coastal habitats, coral reefs, and the surrounding marine environment. The absence of a permanent human population helps preserve much of the island's natural character.

Access to Howland Island is restricted and requires appropriate authorization. Management activities are intended to protect wildlife and maintain the ecological integrity of the island and surrounding waters.
