American Samoa, United States

About American Samoa ▼ +

American Samoa is an unincorporated territory of the United States located in the South Pacific Ocean. It consists of a group of volcanic islands and coral atolls in the central South Pacific, east of Samoa.

Pago Pago is the capital and principal harbor of American Samoa. The territory is known for its dramatic volcanic mountains, tropical forests, coral reefs, coastal villages, beaches, and rich Polynesian cultural heritage.

American Samoa's Status ▼ +

American Samoa is an unincorporated territory of the United States. It is not one of the 50 U.S. states and has a different legal status from incorporated U.S. territories.

American Samoa has its own locally elected government, including a governor and legislature. The territory has a distinctive political and cultural relationship with the United States.

Unlike people born in most other U.S. territories, people born in American Samoa are generally U.S. nationals rather than U.S. citizens at birth, unless they otherwise acquire U.S. citizenship under federal law.

Geography of American Samoa ▼ +

American Samoa consists of several islands and smaller islets in the South Pacific Ocean. The main island, Tutuila, is mountainous and contains steep volcanic slopes, narrow coastal plains, valleys, and sheltered bays.

The territory also includes the Manuʻa Islands, which consist primarily of Ofu, Olosega, and Taʻū, as well as Rose Atoll and Swains Island. The islands differ in their geology, elevation, size, and coastal environments.

American Samoa is part of the Samoan Islands and lies within the tropical South Pacific. Its rugged terrain and surrounding ocean strongly influence settlement, transportation, climate, and natural ecosystems.

Location and Surrounding Waters ▼ +

American Samoa is located in the South Pacific Ocean, approximately 2,600 miles southwest of Hawaii and about 50 miles southeast of the independent nation of Samoa.

The territory is surrounded by tropical Pacific waters containing coral reefs, deep ocean habitats, beaches, rocky coastlines, bays, lagoons, and coastal wetlands.

Its position in the South Pacific places American Samoa within an important region of Polynesia and gives the territory strong connections to neighboring Pacific islands.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in American Samoa is generally identified as Matafao Peak on the island of Tutuila. It rises to approximately 2,142 feet (653 meters) above sea level.

The peak is part of Tutuila's rugged volcanic interior. The island's mountains descend sharply toward the coastline, creating narrow valleys, steep ridges, and dramatic coastal landscapes.

Rivers and Water Resources ▼ +

American Samoa has numerous short streams and small rivers that flow from the mountainous interiors toward the Pacific Ocean. Important waterways include the Fagaʻitua River, Lauliʻi Stream, and other streams draining Tutuila's steep valleys.

Freshwater resources are closely connected to rainfall and groundwater. The territory's tropical forests and watersheds help regulate stream flow and provide important freshwater resources for communities and ecosystems.

Because the islands are relatively small and mountainous, waterways are generally short and can respond quickly to heavy tropical rainfall.

Climate of American Samoa ▼ +

American Samoa has a tropical climate with warm temperatures and high humidity throughout most of the year. The surrounding Pacific Ocean moderates temperatures and contributes abundant moisture.

Rainfall is generally heavy, especially on mountainous areas exposed to prevailing moist winds. The wet tropical climate supports dense forests, streams, wetlands, and lush vegetation.

American Samoa can experience tropical cyclones, heavy rainfall, flooding, landslides, and strong winds. Weather conditions are influenced by large-scale Pacific climate patterns as well as seasonal tropical systems.

Economy of American Samoa ▼ +

American Samoa's economy includes government activity, tuna fishing and processing, retail trade, construction, transportation, services, and small-scale agriculture. The fishing industry has historically been particularly important to the territory's economy.

The surrounding Pacific Ocean supports commercial fishing and marine-related activities. Tourism also contributes to the economy through visitors interested in tropical landscapes, marine environments, hiking, culture, and national park areas.

Agriculture includes crops such as taro, bananas, breadfruit, coconuts, and other tropical products. Many economic activities are influenced by the territory's remote location and limited land area.

Islands and Districts ▼ +

The main islands and island groups of American Samoa include Tutuila, the Manuʻa Islands, Rose Atoll, and Swains Island. Tutuila is the largest and most populated island.

The Manuʻa Islands include Taʻū, Ofu, and Olosega. These islands are characterized by volcanic mountains, steep coastal cliffs, tropical forests, and coral reef ecosystems.

American Samoa is also divided into three main traditional political districts: Eastern District, Western District, and Manuʻa District.

Major Villages and Communities ▼ +

Pago Pago is the capital and an important administrative and commercial center. It is located around a deep natural harbor surrounded by steep volcanic mountains.

Other important villages and communities include Tafuna, Utulei, Fagatogo, Leone, Faleniu, Mapusagafou, and communities throughout the Manuʻa Islands.

Most settlements are concentrated along coastal areas where the terrain is more suitable for housing, transportation, commerce, and community facilities.

Natural Environment ▼ +

American Samoa contains diverse tropical ecosystems, including rainforests, volcanic mountains, streams, wetlands, beaches, mangroves, coral reefs, seagrass habitats, and coastal waters.

The territory contains the National Park of American Samoa, which protects tropical forests, coral reefs, coastal habitats, and important cultural landscapes across parts of Tutuila, Ofu, and Taʻū.

These ecosystems provide habitat for tropical birds, fruit bats, reptiles, marine mammals, fish, corals, sea turtles, seabirds, and many other species. The forests and reefs also provide important ecological and cultural resources for local communities.

Marine Environment ▼ +

The waters around American Samoa contain extensive coral reef ecosystems, lagoons, rocky shores, sandy beaches, and deep Pacific marine habitats.

Coral reefs support numerous species of fish, corals, mollusks, crustaceans, sea turtles, seabirds, and other marine organisms. The reefs also provide food, recreation, coastal protection, and cultural resources.

Marine ecosystems can be affected by tropical cyclones, warming ocean temperatures, coral bleaching, pollution, overfishing, and other environmental pressures.

American Samoa and Samoa ▼ +

American Samoa and Samoa are two politically separate territories in the Samoan Islands. American Samoa is a U.S. territory, while Samoa is an independent country.

The two share strong cultural, linguistic, historical, and geographic connections. They are located relatively close to each other in the South Pacific but have separate governments and political systems.

The main island of American Samoa is Tutuila, while Samoa's largest island is Upolu. Apia is the capital of Samoa, while Pago Pago is the capital of American Samoa.
