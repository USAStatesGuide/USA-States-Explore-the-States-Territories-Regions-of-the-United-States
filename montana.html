Montana, United States

About Montana ▼ +

Montana is a state in the northwestern region of the United States. It is bordered by Canada to the north, North Dakota and South Dakota to the east, Wyoming to the south, and Idaho to the west. The capital of Montana is Helena.

Montana became the 41st state of the United States on November 8, 1889. The state is known for its mountains, wide-open plains, forests, rivers, lakes, and spectacular natural landscapes. It is often called the "Treasure State."

Geography of Montana ▼ +

Montana is defined by the dramatic transition from the Rocky Mountains in the west to the open Great Plains in the east. Western Montana contains several major mountain ranges, including the Bitterroot, Lewis, and Absaroka ranges, with deep valleys, alpine lakes, and rugged peaks. The northwestern part of the state includes Glacier National Park, while the southwestern corner extends into the Yellowstone region. The Continental Divide crosses the state and separates major drainage systems flowing toward the Pacific Ocean and the Gulf of Mexico.

Eastern Montana opens into broad grasslands and rolling plains crossed by the Missouri River and its tributaries. The Missouri, Yellowstone, and Milk rivers form important geographic corridors across this drier region, while badlands and isolated buttes create distinctive landscapes in the southeast. Montana's geography therefore changes from heavily forested mountain ranges and glacial valleys in the west to expansive prairie and high plains in the east.

Montana Borders ▼ +

Montana shares land borders with four U.S. states: North Dakota and South Dakota to the east, Wyoming to the south, and Idaho to the west.

Montana also shares an international border with Canada to the north, bordering the Canadian provinces of British Columbia, Alberta, and Saskatchewan.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in Montana is Granite Peak, which reaches approximately 12,807 feet (3,904 meters) above sea level.

Granite Peak is located in the Beartooth Mountains in southwestern Montana. It is the highest natural point in the state and one of the most prominent mountain peaks in the northern Rocky Mountains.

Rivers and Waterways ▼ +

Montana has an extensive network of rivers and waterways. Major rivers include the Missouri River, Yellowstone River, Clark Fork River, Flathead River, Milk River, Gallatin River, and Blackfoot River.

These waterways are important for ecosystems, agriculture, irrigation, recreation, fishing, wildlife, hydroelectric power, and communities throughout the state.

Economy of Montana ▼ +

Montana's economy has long been connected to its large agricultural and natural-resource base. Cattle ranching is especially important across the eastern plains and western valleys, while wheat, barley, hay, and other crops are grown on the state's broad agricultural lands. Mining and energy production also contribute to the economy, including coal, oil, natural gas, and metals. Forestry remains important in western Montana, where extensive mountain forests support lumber and wood-product industries.

Tourism and outdoor recreation are major economic activities, particularly around Glacier National Park, Yellowstone National Park, mountain communities, rivers, and large wilderness areas. Visitors support hotels, restaurants, guiding, fishing, skiing, and other recreation businesses. Billings is an important regional center for energy, agriculture, healthcare, and trade, while Bozeman has developed as a center for technology, education, tourism, and research. Healthcare, education, construction, and other services also provide employment throughout the state.

Administrative Division ▼ +

Montana is divided into 56 counties. Counties are an important level of local government within the state.

The state also contains cities, towns, municipalities, unincorporated communities, and other local areas. Counties and municipalities provide various local governmental services to residents.

Major Cities ▼ +

Major cities and population centers in Montana include Billings, Missoula, Great Falls, Bozeman, Butte, and Helena.

Helena is the state capital. Billings is the largest city by population and an important commercial and economic center. Missoula is a major university and regional center, while Bozeman has become an important center for education, technology, tourism, and research.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Montana's natural environment changes dramatically from the forested Rocky Mountains in the west to the open grasslands of the Great Plains in the east. The western mountains contain coniferous forests, alpine meadows, glaciers, clear mountain lakes, and cold streams, while the eastern plains are dominated by native grasslands and prairie habitats. The Missouri and Yellowstone rivers create important riparian corridors across the drier eastern landscapes.

Glacier National Park protects rugged mountains, glaciers, alpine habitats, forests, and valleys, while the Montana portion of Yellowstone National Park contains forests, geothermal landscapes, rivers, and high-elevation ecosystems. Montana's wilderness areas provide habitat for elk, grizzly bears, black bears, moose, bighorn sheep, mountain goats, pronghorn, and wolves. Its rivers and lakes also support trout, native fish, waterfowl, and other aquatic species.
