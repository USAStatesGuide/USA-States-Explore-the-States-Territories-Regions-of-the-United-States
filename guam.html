Guam, United States

About Guam ▼ +

Guam is an organized, unincorporated territory of the United States located in the western Pacific Ocean. It is the largest and southernmost island of the Mariana Islands and is an important U.S. military, transportation, and economic center in the western Pacific.

Hagåtña is the capital of Guam, while Dededo is the most populous village. Guam is known for its tropical climate, coral reefs, limestone landscapes, beaches, coastal waters, forests, and distinctive Chamorro cultural heritage.

Guam's Status ▼ +

Guam is an organized, unincorporated territory of the United States. It is not one of the 50 U.S. states.

People born in Guam are generally U.S. citizens under U.S. law. Guam has its own locally elected government, including a governor and legislature, while the United States federal government retains authority over matters established by federal law.

Guam uses the United States dollar as its official currency and operates under a territorial political system within the United States.

Geography of Guam ▼ +

Guam is a relatively small island in the western Pacific Ocean. It is approximately 30 miles long and up to about 8.5 miles wide. The island has a varied landscape that includes limestone plateaus, volcanic terrain, hills, valleys, coastal plains, beaches, and steep coastal cliffs.

Northern Guam is dominated by elevated limestone formations, while central and southern areas contain volcanic rocks and more rugged terrain. The island's highest elevations occur in the southern part of Guam.

Guam is part of the Mariana Islands and lies near the Mariana Trench, one of the deepest oceanic regions on Earth.

Location and Surrounding Waters ▼ +

Guam is located in the western Pacific Ocean, within the Mariana Islands. The island lies east of the Philippines and south of the Northern Mariana Islands.

Guam is surrounded by deep Pacific Ocean waters. Its coastline includes sandy beaches, rocky shores, coral reefs, bays, lagoons, and steep coastal cliffs.

Guam's location gives it strategic importance for transportation, communications, military operations, and connections between the United States and the Asia-Pacific region.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in Guam is Mount Lamlam, located in the southern part of the island. It rises to approximately 1,334 feet (406 meters) above sea level.

Mount Lamlam is part of the rugged volcanic landscape of southern Guam. The southern highlands contain steep slopes, valleys, streams, forests, and areas of volcanic terrain.

Rivers and Water Resources ▼ +

Guam has several streams and rivers that drain the island's interior toward the surrounding Pacific Ocean. Important waterways include the Pago River, Ylig River, Talofofo River, and Umatac River.

Freshwater resources are important because Guam is a small tropical island. Rainfall, surface water, and groundwater contribute to the island's water supply and support wetlands, forests, agriculture, and aquatic ecosystems.

The northern part of Guam contains important groundwater resources within its limestone formations. Groundwater is an important component of the island's freshwater supply.

Climate of Guam ▼ +

Guam has a tropical marine climate characterized by warm temperatures throughout the year. The island generally experiences a wetter season and a drier season rather than the four-season pattern found in many temperate regions.

Rainfall varies across the island, with southern and elevated areas generally receiving more precipitation than some northern locations. Tropical weather systems can bring periods of heavy rainfall and strong winds.

Guam is vulnerable to typhoons. Strong tropical cyclones can produce destructive winds, heavy rain, flooding, and coastal impacts.

Economy of Guam ▼ +

Guam's economy is based primarily on tourism, government activity, military-related activities, construction, retail trade, transportation, and services. Its location in the western Pacific gives it an important role in regional transportation and defense.

Tourism is one of Guam's major economic sectors. Visitors are attracted by tropical beaches, coral reefs, water activities, shopping, cultural attractions, historic sites, and the island's warm climate.

The U.S. military presence is also an important part of Guam's economy. Military facilities and related construction and service industries contribute significantly to employment and economic activity.

Villages and Local Government ▼ +

Guam is divided into 19 villages, which serve as important local communities and administrative areas. These villages are different from counties used in many U.S. states.

Major villages and population centers include Dededo, Yigo, Tamuning, Hagåtña, Mangilao, Barrigada, and Santa Rita.

Guam's territorial government is headed by an elected governor and includes a unicameral legislature known as the Guam Legislature.

Major Urban Areas ▼ +

Hagåtña is the capital and historic political center of Guam. It contains important government buildings, historic sites, cultural institutions, and public facilities.

Dededo is the island's largest village by population and contains extensive residential and commercial development. Tamuning is another major commercial and tourism center.

Other important population centers include Yigo, Mangilao, Barrigada, and Sinajana.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Guam contains diverse tropical environments, including forests, limestone plateaus, wetlands, rivers, beaches, coastal cliffs, mangrove areas, coral reefs, and seagrass habitats.

The island's coral reefs are particularly important marine ecosystems. They provide habitat for fish, corals, crustaceans, sea turtles, and many other marine organisms while also helping protect parts of the coastline from wave energy.

Guam's forests provide habitat for birds, reptiles, mammals, insects, and other organisms. Conservation efforts focus on protecting forests, watersheds, coral reefs, beaches, and other sensitive ecosystems.

Marine Environment ▼ +

Guam is surrounded by a diverse tropical marine environment. Coral reefs, lagoons, seagrass beds, rocky shores, sandy beaches, and deep ocean waters occur around the island.

These marine habitats support a wide variety of organisms, including reef fish, corals, sea turtles, mollusks, crustaceans, seabirds, and other marine wildlife.

Guam's coastal ecosystems are important for biodiversity, recreation, fisheries, tourism, and shoreline protection. They can be affected by storms, coastal development, pollution, invasive species, and other environmental pressures.

Guam and the Mariana Islands ▼ +

Guam is the largest island in the Mariana Islands. The Mariana Islands form an island arc in the western Pacific Ocean and include Guam and the islands of the Northern Mariana Islands.

Guam is politically separate from the Northern Mariana Islands. Guam is an organized, unincorporated U.S. territory, while the Northern Mariana Islands are a separate U.S. commonwealth.

The islands are located in a tectonically active region near the Mariana Trench, which contains the deepest known point in the world's oceans.
