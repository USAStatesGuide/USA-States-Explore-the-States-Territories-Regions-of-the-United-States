Wyoming, United States

About Wyoming ▼ +

Wyoming is a state in the Mountain West region of the United States. It is bordered by Montana to the north, South Dakota and Nebraska to the east, Colorado and Utah to the south, and Idaho and Utah to the west. The capital of Wyoming is Cheyenne.

Wyoming became the 44th state of the United States on July 10, 1890. The state is known for its mountains, high plains, wide-open landscapes, rivers, lakes, and national parks, including Yellowstone and Grand Teton. Wyoming is also known for its important role in the history of the American West.

Geography of Wyoming ▼ +

Wyoming is dominated by high-elevation mountains and broad interior basins that create a distinctive landscape within the Rocky Mountain region. The western part contains the Teton Range and the Absaroka Mountains, while the Wind River Range in central-western Wyoming contains Gannett Peak, the state's highest point. Between the mountain ranges lie large elevated basins and plateaus, including the Green River Basin and the Great Divide Basin, where dry grasslands, sagebrush, and open high-country landscapes are widespread.

Northern Wyoming contains Yellowstone's volcanic plateau, geothermal features, deep canyons, and high mountain landscapes, while Grand Teton National Park is known for the dramatic Teton Range rising sharply above Jackson Hole. Eastern Wyoming transitions into the High Plains, with broad grasslands, isolated mountain ranges, and distinctive formations such as Devils Tower. Major rivers including the Yellowstone, Bighorn, Green, and North Platte drain different parts of the state, creating important valleys and water corridors across an otherwise high and often dry landscape.

Wyoming Borders ▼ +

Wyoming shares land borders with six states: Montana to the north, South Dakota and Nebraska to the east, Colorado and Utah to the south, and Idaho to the west.

Wyoming is completely landlocked and has no coastline. Its location in the interior western United States places it within the Rocky Mountain and Great Plains regions.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in Wyoming is Gannett Peak, which reaches approximately 13,804 feet (4,207 meters) above sea level.

Gannett Peak is located in the Wind River Range in western Wyoming. It is the highest summit in the state and is part of the rugged mountain landscape of the Rocky Mountains.

Rivers and Waterways ▼ +

Wyoming has an extensive network of rivers and waterways. Major rivers include the Yellowstone River, Snake River, Green River, North Platte River, Wind River, and Bighorn River.

These waterways are important for ecosystems, agriculture, drinking water, recreation, wildlife, and communities. Several major rivers originating in Wyoming also contribute to larger river systems that flow across the western United States.

Economy of Wyoming ▼ +

Wyoming's economy is strongly shaped by energy and mineral production. The Powder River Basin in the northeastern part of the state is one of the major coal-producing regions in the United States, while oil and natural gas production are important in several western, central, and southern areas. Wyoming also produces uranium, trona, and other minerals. These resource industries support mining, processing, transportation, equipment, and related services, while coal and natural gas have historically provided significant state revenue.

Tourism and outdoor recreation form another major part of Wyoming's economy, particularly around Yellowstone and Grand Teton national parks, Jackson Hole, and the state's extensive public lands. Visitors come for wildlife viewing, hiking, fishing, skiing, and other mountain activities. Ranching remains important across the high plains and intermountain basins, with cattle and sheep production especially significant. Agriculture also includes hay and other crops grown in irrigated valleys, while transportation, construction, manufacturing, healthcare, and other services support communities across the state.

Administrative Division ▼ +

Wyoming is divided into 23 counties. Counties are an important level of local government within the state.

The state also contains cities, towns, municipalities, unincorporated communities, and other local areas. Counties and municipalities provide various local governmental services to residents.

Major Cities ▼ +

Major cities and population centers in Wyoming include Cheyenne, Casper, Laramie, Gillette, Rock Springs, and Sheridan.

Cheyenne is the state capital and largest city by population. Casper is an important regional center for energy, business, and services. Laramie is home to the University of Wyoming, while Gillette, Rock Springs, and Sheridan are important regional centers for industry, energy, education, and tourism.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Wyoming's natural environment is strongly shaped by its high elevations, mountain ranges, and broad dry basins. The Teton, Wind River, Bighorn, and Absaroka ranges contain conifer forests, alpine meadows, glaciers, rocky peaks, and cold mountain streams, while lower elevations are dominated by sagebrush and open grasslands. Yellowstone contains one of the world's largest concentrations of geothermal features, including geysers, hot springs, and thermal basins created by its underlying volcanic system.

Wyoming's rivers and lakes provide important freshwater habitats, including the Yellowstone, Snake, Green, and North Platte river systems. The state's mountains and plains support large populations of elk, pronghorn, mule deer, bighorn sheep, moose, and other wildlife, while Yellowstone and the Greater Yellowstone ecosystem provide habitat for grizzly bears, wolves, and American bison. Grand Teton and Yellowstone national parks, together with extensive national forests and other public lands, protect large connected landscapes where forests, wetlands, grasslands, rivers, and alpine habitats support diverse ecosystems.
