Hawaii, United States

About Hawaii ▼ +

Hawaii is a U.S. state located in the central Pacific Ocean. It is an archipelago made up of eight major islands and many smaller islets. Hawaii is the southernmost state of the United States and is separated from the continental United States by the Pacific Ocean. The capital of Hawaii is Honolulu.

Hawaii became the 50th state of the United States on August 21, 1959. The state is known for its volcanic landscapes, tropical forests, beaches, coral reefs, mountains, valleys, waterfalls, and diverse coastal environments.

Geography of Hawaii ▼ +

Hawaii is an archipelago in the central Pacific Ocean formed by volcanic activity over a long-lived hotspot. Its eight main islands have very different landscapes, ranging from the broad volcanic slopes of Hawaii Island to the steep, deeply eroded mountains of Kauai. Hawaii Island contains Mauna Kea and Mauna Loa, two of the world's most prominent volcanic mountains, while Maui is dominated by the large volcanic mass of Haleakala.

The islands also contain sharp ridges, deep valleys, lava fields, waterfalls, tropical forests, and narrow coastal plains. Kauai is particularly known for the deeply carved Waimea Canyon and the dramatic Na Pali Coast, while Oahu combines volcanic mountain ranges with a broad coastal plain around Honolulu. The surrounding Pacific waters are an essential part of Hawaii's geography, separating the islands and creating extensive beaches, reefs, bays, and rugged volcanic coastlines.

Hawaii Borders ▼ +

Hawaii has no international or land borders with other U.S. states. The state is completely surrounded by the Pacific Ocean.

Hawaii is located in the central Pacific Ocean, thousands of miles from the continental United States. Its islands extend across a large area of the North Pacific.

Highest Point ▼ +

The highest point in Hawaii is Mauna Kea, which rises approximately 13,796 feet (4,205 meters) above sea level.

Mauna Kea is located on the island of Hawaii. It is a dormant volcano and one of the highest mountains in the Pacific region when measured from sea level.

Rivers and Waterways ▼ +

Hawaii does not have large continental-scale river systems, but its mountainous islands contain numerous streams and short rivers. Important waterways include the Wailuku River, Wailua River, Hanalei River, and Wailoa River.

These waterways are important for freshwater resources, ecosystems, agriculture, wetlands, recreation, and the communities located throughout the Hawaiian Islands.

Economy of Hawaii ▼ +

Hawaii's economy is strongly shaped by its location in the Pacific and its distance from the U.S. mainland. Tourism is the leading private economic activity, supported by resorts, hotels, restaurants, air transportation, cruises, and outdoor recreation. Oahu is the main commercial center, with Honolulu serving as the state's principal hub for business, government, transportation, and international connections across the Pacific.

Hawaii also has distinctive agricultural industries, including Kona coffee, macadamia nuts, tropical fruits, flowers, and seed crops. The state's military presence is another major economic factor, with large U.S. military installations on Oahu supporting employment and related services. Because Hawaii imports much of its food, fuel, and other goods, its ports and airports are essential to the economy. Renewable energy, including solar, wind, and geothermal power, is also important as the islands work to reduce their dependence on imported fossil fuels.

Administrative Division ▼ +

Hawaii is divided into five county-equivalent areas: Hawaii County, Honolulu County, Kalawao County, Kauai County, and Maui County.

Honolulu County includes the island of Oahu and is formally organized as the City and County of Honolulu. The other counties cover the remaining major islands and island groups of the state.

Major Cities ▼ +

Major population centers in Hawaii include Honolulu, Hilo, Pearl City, Kailua, Waipahu, and Kaneohe.

Honolulu is the state capital and the largest urban center in Hawaii. Hilo is the largest population center on the island of Hawaii, while other major communities are located mainly on Oahu and the neighboring islands.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Hawaii's natural environment has been shaped by volcanic activity, tropical climate, and extreme isolation in the Pacific Ocean. The islands contain native Hawaiian forests, volcanic slopes, high-elevation ecosystems, valleys, and coastal wetlands. Many species evolved in isolation, making Hawaii home to a large number of plants, birds, and other organisms found nowhere else in the world. Native forests include distinctive species such as koa and ohia lehua.

Hawaii's surrounding waters contain coral reefs, seagrass areas, rocky shores, and deep marine habitats that support green sea turtles, Hawaiian monk seals, reef fish, and humpback whales during their seasonal migrations. Some of the state's most distinctive environments occur where volcanic land meets the ocean, including lava coastlines, black-sand beaches, and sheltered bays. The combination of isolated islands, active volcanoes, native forests, and tropical marine ecosystems gives Hawaii a highly distinctive natural environment.
