About Baker Island ▼ +

Baker Island is an uninhabited coral island in the central Pacific Ocean. It is an unincorporated, unorganized territory of the United States and forms part of the United States Minor Outlying Islands.

The island has no permanent human population and has no towns, cities, or local government. Baker Island is protected as part of the Baker Island National Wildlife Refuge and is surrounded by important marine habitats. Its remote location makes it one of the more isolated U.S. islands in the Pacific.

Geography of Baker Island ▼ +

Baker Island is a small, low-lying coral island located in the central Pacific Ocean. It is approximately halfway between Hawaii and Australia and lies near the equator. The island is surrounded by a shallow reef and has a narrow coastal zone.

The island is mostly flat and consists primarily of coral sand, reef limestone, and other materials produced by its tropical marine environment. Baker Island has no mountains, permanent rivers, or large freshwater bodies. Its small size and low elevation make it particularly vulnerable to changes in sea level and extreme weather.

Location and Surrounding Area ▼ +

Baker Island is located in the central Pacific Ocean, close to the equator. It is situated approximately 3,090 kilometers (1,920 miles) southwest of Honolulu, Hawaii.

The island has no land borders because it is completely surrounded by the Pacific Ocean. Its nearest neighboring U.S. island territories include Howland Island and other remote Pacific islands. The surrounding ocean is an important part of Baker Island's geographic and ecological environment.

Highest Point ▼ +

Baker Island is extremely low-lying. Its highest natural elevation is approximately 26 feet (8 meters) above sea level.

Unlike mountainous Pacific islands, Baker Island has no significant hills or elevated interior areas. Its low elevation and small land area are important characteristics of its coral-island environment.

Waterways and Coastal Areas ▼ +

Baker Island has no permanent rivers, streams, or lakes. Because of its small size and porous coral geology, rainfall rapidly infiltrates the ground and there are no major surface freshwater systems.

The island is surrounded by coral reefs, shallow coastal waters, and deeper Pacific Ocean waters. These marine environments provide important habitat for fish, corals, seabirds, sea turtles, and other wildlife.

Human Activity and Economy ▼ +

Baker Island has no permanent residents and does not have a conventional local economy. There are no towns, commercial centers, hotels, or permanent business activities on the island.

Human activity is limited and is primarily associated with wildlife management, scientific research, conservation, and occasional authorized visits. The island's protected status helps preserve its terrestrial and marine ecosystems and limits development.

Administrative Status ▼ +

Baker Island is an unincorporated, unorganized territory of the United States. It is administered as part of the United States Minor Outlying Islands rather than as a state, commonwealth, or organized U.S. territory.

The island is federally managed and is protected as the Baker Island National Wildlife Refuge. Because there is no permanent population, Baker Island does not have municipalities, counties, cities, or local elected government.

Settlements and Population ▼ +

Baker Island has no permanent population and has no officially established cities or towns. There are no permanent residential communities on the island.

The island has been visited by people at various points in its history, including workers associated with former settlement and guano activities. Today, access is highly restricted and human presence is generally limited to authorized scientific, conservation, and management activities.

Natural Environment ▼ +

Baker Island supports a distinctive tropical island ecosystem despite its very small size. Vegetation is adapted to the dry, exposed conditions of a low coral island, and the island provides important nesting and resting habitat for numerous seabirds.

The surrounding marine environment contains coral reefs, fish, sea turtles, and other ocean wildlife. Baker Island is particularly important for seabird populations and other marine-dependent species. Its protection as a national wildlife refuge helps conserve both terrestrial habitats and the surrounding marine ecosystem.

Conservation ▼ +

Baker Island is protected as part of the Baker Island National Wildlife Refuge. The island and its surrounding waters provide important habitat for seabirds, shorebirds, and marine species.

Conservation efforts focus on protecting seabird nesting colonies, native coastal habitats, coral reefs, and the surrounding marine ecosystem. The island's lack of a permanent human population helps reduce direct disturbance.

Access to Baker Island is restricted and requires appropriate authorization. Conservation management helps protect its fragile terrestrial and marine ecosystems for future generations.
